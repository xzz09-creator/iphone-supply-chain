Section 01

Where does $999 go?

Click any row to learn more about each stakeholder.

Apple (brand + design)
$450
Apple captures value through intellectual property — the A18 chip design, iOS, and brand prestige. None of this involves physical manufacturing. Apple's gross margin on iPhone is ~43%, far exceeding any supplier in its chain.
Components (global)
$300
Chips, screens, cameras, batteries, and structural parts sourced from Taiwan, South Korea, and China. Despite being the most technically complex part of production, suppliers earn thin margins due to intense competition and Apple's negotiating power.
Assembly (Foxconn)
$30
Foxconn assembles iPhones in Zhengzhou and Shenzhen. Despite employing hundreds of thousands of workers, assembly captures only ~3% of the retail price — the clearest example of the smile curve in action.
Retail + logistics + tax
$219
Distribution, retail margins, import duties, and logistics costs. This share varies significantly by country and sales channel.

Section 02

The smile curve

Value-added in a supply chain forms a U-shape — highest at R&D and branding, lowest at manufacturing. Apple owns both ends. Suppliers are stuck in the middle.

Value is high at R&D and Marketing stages, lowest at Assembly.

Section 04

Buyer power and the supplier's dilemma

When one buyer controls most of your revenue, they control your pricing. Porter's Five Forces calls this buyer bargaining power — and it explains why Shenzhen's smartest manufacturers have deliberately avoided dependence on any single client.

Single-client dependence
If Apple represents 70%+ of revenue, a supplier must accept Apple's annual price-down demands. Losing the contract is existential.
Negotiating leverage
Revenue risk
Diversified client base
By serving Apple, Huawei, Samsung, and others simultaneously, suppliers can credibly walk away from bad terms — and Apple knows it.
